1. **Solve the problem yourself first, in writing, before you read the solution's numbers.** From the problem statement alone, state what each unknown denotes, which relation the statement asserts, what is actually being asked, and carry the arithmetic through to a final answer. Record your intermediate values as a numbered list with units. Only then read the solution; reading it first anchors you to its framing and is the main reason whole classes of mistakes stay invisible.

2. **Audit every printed line in a table, and give each one a verdict.** For each line of the solution write: the inputs it uses, the operation it performs, the value your own derivation gives, the value printed, and PASS or FAIL. No line may be skipped as "standard algebra", "just restating", or "obviously fine", and the last lines get the same scrutiny as the first. A line is PASS only after you have recomputed it; it is never PASS because it looked routine. Do not resolve a mismatch by assuming the solution rounded, simplified, or took an equivalent route — verify that by exact recomputation.

3. **Recompute each step blind, before reading its printed result.** Compute what the step should yield from its own stated inputs, then look at what was written. Never read the result and ask whether it "looks reasonable": a swapped operation, a reversed operand order, a reciprocal, an inverted relation, or a dropped remainder all produce plausible numbers.

4. **Run a separate semantic pass in which arithmetic correctness counts for nothing.** For each line, write the English sentence it asserts, then name the operation and each operand together with the phrase in the problem it came from. Check against the statement, re-reading the defining sentence rather than the solution's paraphrase: is this the operation the wording forces; for a non-commutative operation, are the operands in the order the statement dictates; does each symbol still denote what it was defined to denote; is this the case, term, index, or period asked about; is the intended quantity in the numerator; does this substituted number appear in the problem at all? Lines whose numbers are internally consistent but whose meaning is wrong pass every arithmetic check and can only be caught here.

5. **Decide additive versus multiplicative from the problem, then check the solution used the same one.** Wherever a quantity changes, scales, is shared, or is compared, determine from the statement whether the relation preserves a difference or a ratio (rates, percentages, per-unit costs, proportions, scaling, interest, inverse variation are multiplicative; constant increments are additive). Adding where the problem fixes a ratio, or multiplying where it fixes a difference, yields ordinary-looking numbers — catch it by re-deriving the relation, not by inspecting the result. For an inverse relation, confirm the product of the paired quantities is held constant, not their ratio.

6. **Compute exactly; treat convenient roundness as a red flag.** Whenever a division, average, percentage, or split lands on a whole number or a tidy figure, redo it exactly. Silent rounding, truncation, a dropped remainder, a fraction replaced by a nearby integer, or a non-integer snapped to whole units is an error unless the problem's context explicitly requires whole units — and if it does, check the direction the context demands. Recompute fraction steps with numerator *and* denominator both transformed, and check signs and decimal placement against a rough estimate.

7. **Keep a ledger of given quantities and check completeness against the question.** List every number and condition in the statement and mark where the solution uses each: a given never used, one applied twice where it applies once (or once where it applies repeatedly), a value carried forward where a new one was required, and a number not derivable from the problem are all errors. Then enumerate every quantity, part, case, or unit the question demands; a chain that stops at an intermediate value, handles one case and drops another, or answers a related-but-different question is an error at the line where it diverges.

8. **Localise by earliest divergence, then re-base and keep going; expect several independent errors.** Align intermediate values in order and find the first that differs. Then adopt the solution's value there and continue comparing downstream, so a later independent mistake is still exposed rather than hidden behind the first. Repeat to the end. Finding one defect raises rather than lowers the chance of another, and they are usually non-adjacent; never end the audit because you have a strong candidate or a comfortable number of claims. Matching final answers do not clear the work — compensating mistakes and errors in an unasked-for part both occur.

9. **Separate root cause from consequence by an explicit test.** Recompute each suspect line using the values the solution itself produced upstream, and write that recomputation out. If the line follows correctly from those inputs it is a symptom of an earlier defect and gets no claim, however wrong its number looks; mention it inside the earlier claim. If it fails on its own inputs it earns a claim even when an earlier line was also wrong. If your explanation would say the number is wrong "because of" or "stems from" an earlier value, you have written a consequence — delete it.

10. **One claim per error, one error per claim.** Emit exactly one claim for each FAIL and none for any PASS. Two independent mistakes in one sentence or equation chain get two claims with distinct, non-overlapping spans; a single conceptual defect that shows up in several places gets one claim, placed where the wrong quantity, operation, or interpretation is first written down, never at a later restatement of the same mistake.

11. **Gate each claim on a finished re-derivation, and check your spans before submitting.** In two to four sentences state what the line asserts, what the correct derivation gives concretely, and why the line is wrong; if your write-up hedges, self-corrects, or concedes the line may be right, resolve it away from the page and either state the finished contradiction or drop the claim. Markup, notation, spelling, alternative-but-valid methods, and harmless redundancy are not errors. Every span must be an exact contiguous substring of the solution, copied character for character including digits, symbols, and spacing, and no longer than the clause or equation carrying the mistake — locate each span in the solution text one final time, and re-read your finished response to confirm it is well-formed and contains nothing beyond the required claim structure.
