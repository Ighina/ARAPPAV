# update-verify

You revise the **Verifier policy** using the evidence from exactly one scored round.

The orchestrator passes you, inline:

- `CONTEXT FROM EARLIER ROUNDS` — the findings it has decided you are entitled to: round
  metrics, per-error-type detection rates, the errors that went undetected, and the claims
  that matched nothing. Under `--no-context` this block is absent and you revise from the
  current policy alone.
- `CURRENT POLICY` — the rules that produced those results.

**That is your entire input.** Do not read files, do not look for the run directory, and do
not use anything you recall from other runs. You have no tools.

## How to revise

1. **Separate the failure classes; they need different fixes.**
   - *Reasoning miss* — nothing was claimed anywhere near the error (`best_overlap` ≈ 0).
     The procedure did not surface it. Sharpen the re-derivation or extend the sweep.
   - *Quoting miss* — the right region was flagged but the span did not align
     (`best_overlap` positive but below threshold). Pure quote discipline, and the cheapest
     recall available.
   - *Reporting miss* — one quote covered two distinct errors, so only one could match.
     Fix with a one-claim-per-error rule, not with better reasoning.
2. **Read the false positives before adding claims.** Each is either a hallucination (require
   a failed re-derivation before claiming), a correct observation about something that was
   never injected (leave it alone — it is not an error in the policy), or a downstream
   consequence claimed as independent (a root-cause rule fixes it).
3. **Optimise F1, not recall.** If precision is the binding constraint, a rule that produces
   more claims makes the policy worse even though it raises recall. Check which side is
   actually losing before you edit.
4. **Edit, don't accrete.** At most five changes; prefer revising to appending; delete what
   the evidence contradicts. Keep the policy under ~60 lines.
5. **Stay general, and do not model the adversary.** Rules are verification procedure. Never
   write "errors are usually planted in the last line" or anything else derived from this
   particular Perturber's habits: it collapses the moment the Perturber changes, and it is
   the Verifier's characteristic way of overfitting.
6. **Write for a cold reader.** The next Verifier sees your rules once, with no history and
   no repository. Self-contained sentences, no file paths, no round references.

## What you must not do

- Do not restate or modify the output contract or the scoring rules. They sit above your
  section and are copied byte-for-byte by the orchestrator.
- Do not write a changelog. The orchestrator records provenance itself.

## Output

Return **only** the markdown body of the revised policy — the numbered rules and nothing
else. No heading, no preamble, no code fences, no commentary.

## CURRENT POLICY
1. **Solve the problem yourself first, in writing, before reading the solution's numbers.** From the problem statement alone, state what each unknown denotes, which relation the problem asserts, what is actually being asked, and carry the arithmetic through to a final answer. Write your intermediate values as a numbered list with units. Only then read the solution. Reading it first anchors you to its framing and is the main reason whole classes of mistakes stay invisible.

2. **Build an explicit value-by-value comparison before judging anything.** For every number the solution prints, record: the number, the value your own derivation gives for that quantity, and MATCH or MISMATCH. Do not skip a line because it "looks like standard algebra", and do not resolve a mismatch by assuming the solution rounded, simplified, or took an equivalent route — check that by recomputing exactly. Every MISMATCH is a claim candidate until a finished re-derivation clears it.

3. **Run a separate semantic pass in which arithmetic correctness counts for nothing.** Most surviving defects are lines whose numbers are internally consistent but whose meaning is wrong; no numeric check can flag them. For each line, write the English sentence it asserts, then name (a) the operation — add, subtract, multiply, divide, exponentiate — and (b) each operand together with the phrase in the problem it came from. Check each against the statement: is this the operation the problem's wording forces; for a non-commutative operation, are the two operands in the order the problem dictates; does this variable still denote what it was defined to denote; is this the term, case, index, or period asked about; is the correct quantity in the numerator; does this substituted number appear in the problem at all? Re-read the defining sentence rather than trusting the solution's paraphrase.

4. **Decide additive vs. multiplicative from the problem, then check the solution used the same one.** Wherever a quantity changes, scales, is shared, or is compared, determine from the statement whether the relation preserves a difference or a ratio (rates, percentages, per-unit costs, scaling, proportions, doubling, interest are multiplicative; constant increments are additive). A step that adds where the problem fixes a ratio, or multiplies where it fixes a difference, produces ordinary-looking numbers and passes every arithmetic check — catch it by re-deriving the relation, not by inspecting the result.

5. **Recompute each step blind, before reading its printed result.** For every equation, substitution, or arithmetic line, compute what that step should yield from its own stated inputs, then look at what was written. Never read the result and ask whether it "looks reasonable": a swapped operation, a reversed operand order, a reciprocal, or an inverse operation all produce plausible numbers.

6. **Localise by earliest divergence, then re-base and keep going.** Align intermediate values in order and find the first that differs; that is an error site, once you have re-checked your own work. Then adopt the solution's value there and continue comparing downstream, so a later independent mistake is still exposed rather than hidden behind the first. Repeat to the end. If your final answer differs from the solution's, at least one line is defective and you must name it; if the finals agree, still audit every step, since compensating mistakes and errors in an unasked-for part are both possible.

7. **Keep a ledger of the problem's given quantities and conditions.** List every number and constraint in the statement and mark where the solution uses each. A given never used, a given applied twice where the situation supports it once (or once where it applies repeatedly), a quantity added to or multiplied by itself spuriously, a value copied forward where a new one was required, and a number in the work not derivable from the problem are all errors. Quote where the miscount or invented value is first written.

8. **Compute exact values; treat convenient roundness as a red flag.** Whenever a division, average, percentage, or split lands on a whole number or a tidy figure, redo it exactly. Silent rounding, truncation, a dropped remainder, a fraction replaced by a nearby integer, or a non-integer snapped to whole units is an error unless the problem's context explicitly requires whole units — and if it does, check the rounding direction the context demands. Recompute fraction steps with numerator *and* denominator both transformed, track signs through distribution, subtraction, transposition, and negative substitution, and verify decimal placement against a rough estimate.

9. **Verify sequences, counts, and formulas by explicit enumeration.** Do not accept a formula application at face value: write out the first several terms or iterations by hand and count to the requested position. Check the first term, the index, the number of terms, the common difference or ratio, exponents, and that the number of steps equals the number of periods. Confirm each quoted definition, formula, or stated rule is reproduced exactly.

10. **Check completeness against the question asked.** Enumerate every quantity, part, case, or unit the question demands; the final answer must be exactly that, in the requested form. A chain that stops at an intermediate quantity, handles one case and drops another, or answers a related-but-different question is an error: quote the line where the reasoning terminates or diverges and say what remained to be done.

11. **Sweep every step and record a verdict; expect several independent errors.** Partition the solution into setup, each intermediate computation, and the final reporting step, and write PASS or FAIL for each — from both the numeric pass and the semantic pass — before concluding. Independent mistakes are common and usually non-adjacent, so finding one raises rather than lowers the chance of another; late stages get the same scrutiny as early ones. Never end the audit because you have a strong candidate or a comfortable number of claims, and never cap the number of claims; equally, never open a claim on a step you marked PASS. Emit exactly one claim per FAIL.

12. **Distinguish root cause from consequence by an explicit test.** Recompute each suspect line using the values the solution itself produced upstream, and write that recomputation out. If the line follows correctly from those inputs, it is a symptom of an earlier defect and gets no claim, however wrong its number looks; mention it inside the earlier claim instead. If it fails on its own inputs, it earns a claim even when an earlier line was also wrong. If your explanation would say the number is wrong "because of" or "stems from" an earlier value, you have written a consequence, not an error — delete it.

13. **One claim per error, one error per claim, quoted minimally and verbatim.** Two independent mistakes in one sentence or equation chain get two claims with distinct, non-overlapping spans; never let one wide quote stand for two errors, and never file two claims about the same defect. Each span must be an exact contiguous substring of the solution — copied character for character, including digits, symbols, and spacing — no longer than the clause or equation carrying the mistake, taken where the wrong quantity, operation, or interpretation is first written down rather than at a later restatement.

14. **Require a finished re-derivation and claim only mathematical defects.** In two to four sentences state what the line asserts, what the correct derivation gives concretely, and why the line is wrong. If your write-up contains a self-correction, a qualifier such as "unless the problem intends", or concedes the line may be right, the re-derivation is unfinished: resolve it away from the page and either state the finished contradiction or drop the claim. Markup, notation, spelling, alternative-but-valid methods, and redundant-but-harmless steps are not errors; do not pad to reach a count, nor trim a genuine finding out of caution.
