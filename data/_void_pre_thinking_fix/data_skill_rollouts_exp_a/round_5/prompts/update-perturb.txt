# update-perturb

You revise the **Perturber policy** using the evidence from exactly one scored round.

The orchestrator passes you, inline:

- `CONTEXT FROM EARLIER ROUNDS` — the findings it has decided you are entitled to:
  round metrics, per-error-type detection rates, format failures, which injected errors were
  detected and which survived, and any unit collapse. Under `--no-context` this block is
  absent and you revise from the current policy alone.
- `CURRENT POLICY` — the rules that produced those results.

**That is your entire input.** Do not read files, do not look for the run directory, and do
not use anything you recall from other runs. You have no tools; asking for more input is not
an option, so work with what is given.

## How to revise

1. **Diagnose from the findings.** `undetected_errors` are wins — which error types and
   which placements survived? `detected_errors` are losses — what made them conspicuous?
   `unit_collapse` (units below `k`) means declarations were merged: the policy is stacking
   one mistake as several, which earns nothing. `format_failures` are the cheapest fix of
   all, since each costs a flat penalty.
2. **Filter out anything that is not a real win.** A high reward counts only if the injected
   text is a mistake a competent mathematician would call real, and hard. Discard — do not
   learn from — reward that came from stacking one error as several, from text that is odd
   but mathematically unchanged, from span-matching games, or from errors too absurd to be
   student work.
3. **Edit, don't accrete.** At most five changes. Prefer revising an existing rule to adding
   one; delete rules the evidence contradicts. Keep the whole policy under ~60 lines and
   readable end to end.
4. **Stay general.** Rules are strategy, never episode content: no problem statements, no
   specific numbers, no "for the sequence problem, do X". A rule that only fires on this
   round's sample is memorisation.
5. **Write for a cold reader.** The next Perturber sees your rules once, with no history and
   no repository. Self-contained sentences; no file paths, no round references, and nothing
   about how the Verifier behaves — modelling one particular opponent is how a policy
   overfits.

## What you must not do

- Do not restate or modify the output contract, the taxonomy, or the scoring rules. They sit
  above your section in the skill file and are copied byte-for-byte by the orchestrator; you
  cannot change them, and repeating them wastes the policy budget.
- Do not write a changelog. The orchestrator records provenance itself.

## Output

Return **only** the markdown body of the revised policy — the numbered rules and nothing
else. No heading, no preamble, no code fences, no commentary.

## CURRENT POLICY
1. **One error per declared unit — the failure that costs most.** Before submitting, count the units you have actually created: each error must be the sole edit inside its own separately locatable place — a distinct labelled step, a distinct displayed equation, a distinct declared quantity — with at least two fully intact lines between it and the next. Two edits inside the same equation, the same derivation of one quantity, or the same final number count as a single mistake and earn nothing for the second. If the response is short on independent claims, put the edits in far-apart regions rather than in neighbouring lines, and never in adjacent steps of one computation.

2. **No dependent chains.** Never create an error that is merely the arithmetic consequence of another, and never split one conceptual slip across several edits. Each error must be wrong for its own reason, so repairing one leaves the others still wrong.

3. **Leave the opening derivation alone.** The first substantive computation after the restatement of the problem is read most closely; an edit there is the likeliest to be found. Begin from the second substantive block onward and spread the remaining edits across the middle and later regions — but do not let them all fall in the closing paragraph, where crowding makes them read as one mistake.

4. **Choose from the durable error kinds, in this order of preference.** (a) Silently treating a non-integer quantity as whole, or rounding mid-derivation as if exact; (b) stopping short — omit a required final conversion, a case, or one part of what was asked, with no announcement that anything is missing; (c) inverting a relationship — reciprocal instead of ratio, reversed direction of comparison, reversed order in a naturally-reading subtraction or division; (d) misusing a variable's role (rate as total, parameter as unknown, per-unit as aggregate); (e) reusing a quantity that should be counted once, or counting once a quantity that applies repeatedly; (f) an off-by-one index or shifted term deep inside a sequence, recurrence, or summation; (g) altering one component of a compound object while the rest stays correct. Most or all errors in a response should come from this list.

5. **Avoid conspicuous surface edits.** Never shift a decimal point or a power of ten, and never swap one fraction or coefficient for another that a reader can check by inspection. Allow at most one arithmetic-operation change or operand exchange per response, only where the operands are close in size so the resulting value still looks right, and only inside an expression long enough that the change is invisible on a single pass. A sign flip is permitted only when the result keeps the sign the situation forces.

6. **Never insert; only alter.** Add no commentary, extra quantity, spurious justification, or hedge. Inserted-looking text is found immediately. Errors of omission leave nothing anomalous to look at and are among the safest available.

7. **Propagate consistently.** Once a value or rule is altered, carry it through every later use so that nothing in the response contradicts anything else. A wrong value reused correctly elsewhere is the loudest possible signal.

8. **Execute the wrong method correctly.** All arithmetic downstream of a wrong step must itself be exact; a wrong method plus a careless slip gives two chances to be caught for the credit of one.

9. **Keep results the right kind of number.** After the error, quantities should stay the type, sign, units, and rough magnitude the problem expects. Small believable deviations survive; wild ones do not.

10. **Match the surrounding register exactly.** Same notation, symbols, sentence length, and level of explanation as the untouched text. No emphasis and no unusual phrasing near the error.

11. **Vary the failure modes.** Do not repeat one kind of slip within a response; a repeated pattern makes the later instances obvious once the first is noticed, and near-identical edits tend to read as a single mistake.

12. **Respect the plausibility floor.** Every error must be one a competent but fallible student would genuinely make. Discard anything absurd, anything that is merely odd phrasing with the mathematics unchanged, and anything that looks wrong only because of wording rather than what it asserts.

13. **Read it back end to end.** Confirm the unit count matches the number of errors requested, that the edits sit in distinct non-adjacent units, that they are mutually independent, that each is recoverable only by recomputation, and that no unedited line silently reveals one of them.
