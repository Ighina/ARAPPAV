1. **Build your own solution first, in writing, before reading the given one critically.** From the problem statement alone, state what each unknown denotes, which relation the problem asserts, what the question actually asks for, and then carry the arithmetic through to a final answer. Only after that do you compare, line by line. Reading the solution first anchors you to its framing and is the main reason whole classes of mistakes stay invisible.

2. **Assume some errors are internally consistent, and hunt them by premise, not by arithmetic.** A wrong setup propagates cleanly: every later step follows correctly from it, so no arithmetic check will ever flag it. For each line ask what it *claims about the problem*, not whether it computes correctly: does this variable still denote what the problem said it denotes; is this the relation the problem states (a difference where a ratio is required, a sum where a product is, a rate where a total is); is this the term, index, case, or period asked about; is the right operand playing the right role in a non-commutative operation; does this substituted number appear in the problem at all? Verify each premise against the problem text directly, re-reading the sentence that defines it.

3. **Check the direction of every relation, rate, and inverse.** For each division, ratio, per-unit quantity, "per", "of", "times as many", "faster/slower", "increase/decrease by", or unit conversion, decide from the problem which quantity belongs on top and which operation is required, then confirm the line matches. Reciprocals, an inverse operation used in place of the intended one, and solving for a quantity when it should have been applied (or vice versa) produce plausible numbers and must be tested by explicit recomputation, not eyeballed.

4. **Keep a ledger of the problem's given quantities.** List every number and condition in the statement, then mark where the solution uses each. A given never used, a given used twice where the situation supports it once (or once where it applies repeatedly), a quantity added to itself or carried into a step it does not belong in, and a number appearing in the work but not in the problem are all errors. Quote the line where the miscount or invented value is first written.

5. **Audit every stage to the end and sign each one off.** Partition the solution into interpretation/setup, each intermediate computation, and the final reporting step. Work through them in order and record a verdict for every stage before you finish. Finding a mistake raises, not lowers, the likelihood that another sits in a different stage: independent mistakes are usually non-adjacent, and late stages are audited as carefully as early ones. Never end the audit because you already have a strong candidate or a comfortable number of claims — end it only when every stage has been checked.

6. **Test each line against its own inputs, and re-test lines you have written off as consequences.** Recompute every line using the values the solution itself produced upstream. If it follows correctly from those inputs, it is a symptom of an earlier defect and gets no claim, however wrong its number looks — a conclusion that merely inherits an upstream wrong value is not an independent error, and saying so belongs in the earlier claim. If it fails on its own inputs, it earns a claim even when an earlier line was also wrong: a line can be both downstream of one error and independently defective.

7. **Run an explicit category checklist on every line.** Is the operation right (×, ÷, +, −, powers, roots and their inverses)? Are the operands in the right order? Is each definition, formula, or stated rule reproduced exactly? For sequences, counts, and iterations: are the first term, index, exponent, number of terms, and common difference/ratio each right, and does the count of steps match the count of periods?

8. **Micro-audit fractions, signs, decimals, and exactness separately.** Recompute every fraction operation with numerator *and* denominator both transformed correctly — a step that changes only one of them is a standing error. Track the sign of every term through distribution, subtraction, transposition, and negative substitution. Check decimal placement against a rough estimate. Treat any silent rounding, truncation, dropped remainder, or non-integer snapped to a whole number as an error unless the problem's context explicitly requires whole units; whenever a division or average comes out suspiciously even, recompute the exact value yourself.

9. **Check completeness against the question asked.** Re-read the question and enumerate every quantity, part, case, or unit it demands. The final answer must be exactly that, in the requested form. A chain that stops after an intermediate quantity, handles one case and drops another, or answers a related-but-different question is an error: quote the line where the reasoning terminates or diverges, and say what remained to be done.

10. **One claim per error, one error per claim.** If two independent mistakes appear in the same sentence or equation chain, emit two claims with distinct, non-overlapping minimal spans. Never let one wide quote stand for two errors, and never file two claims about the same defect.

11. **Quote minimally and verbatim, at the point of introduction.** The span must be an exact contiguous substring of the solution, no longer than the clause or equation carrying the mistake, and long enough to be unique. Quote where the wrong quantity, operation, or interpretation is first written down — not a later restatement, and never a merge of adjacent sentences.

12. **Require a finished re-derivation and an unhedged explanation.** In two to four sentences state what the line asserts, what the correct derivation gives concretely, and why the line is wrong. If your explanation needs "however", "unless the problem intends", or any other qualifier to stand up, the re-derivation is unfinished: resolve it or drop the claim. A suspicion without a produced correct value or correct relation is not a claim.

13. **Do not claim non-mathematical defects.** Markup, notation style, spelling, spacing, alternative-but-valid methods, redundant-but-harmless steps, and presentation that does not change a value are not errors. Claim only when a value, an operation, an interpretation, or a logical inference is wrong.

14. **Report every step that failed re-derivation on its own terms, and nothing else.** Do not pad to reach a count, and do not trim a genuine late-stage finding out of caution because you have already reported earlier ones.
