1. **Build your own solution first, in writing, before reading the given one critically.** From the problem statement alone, state what each unknown denotes, which relation the problem asserts, and then carry the arithmetic through to a final answer. Only after that do you compare, line by line. Reading the solution first anchors you to its framing and is the main reason whole classes of mistakes stay invisible.

2. **Assume some errors are internally consistent, and hunt them by premise, not by arithmetic.** A wrong setup propagates cleanly: every later step follows correctly from it, so no arithmetic check will ever flag it. For each line ask what it *claims about the problem*, not just whether it computes correctly: does this variable still mean what the problem said it means; is this the relation the problem states (a difference where a ratio is required, a sum where a product is, a rate where a total is); is this the term, index, or period the question asks about; is this the right operand playing the right role in a non-commutative operation; does this substituted number appear in the problem statement at all? Verify each such premise against the problem text directly.

3. **Sweep to the end and spread your attention across the whole solution.** Audit the setup and interpretation sentences, the middle of the chain, and the concluding steps as three separate passes. Independent mistakes are common and rarely adjacent, so finding one tells you nothing about the rest. Never let two claims land inside the same short computation chain: if you have two candidates there, at most one of them is an independent error.

4. **Kill consequences before you claim.** For every candidate line, recompute it using the values the solution itself had already produced upstream. If it follows correctly from those inputs, it is a symptom of an earlier defect and gets no claim, no matter how wrong its number is. Claim the earliest line that is wrong on its own terms — the place where a wrong value, wrong relation, or wrong interpretation *first appears* — not the later line that merely carries it. A wrong final answer alone is never a claim; the boxed value earns a claim only when the work above it was right and the answer misreports or mis-selects it.

5. **Run an explicit category checklist on every line.** Is the operation right (×, ÷, +, −, powers, and their inverses)? Are the operands in the right order? Was a quantity solved for when it should have been applied? Is each definition, formula, or given rule reproduced exactly? For sequences, counts, and iterations: are the first term, index, exponent, number of terms, and common difference/ratio each right, and does the count of steps match the count of periods?

6. **Micro-audit fractions, signs, decimals, and exactness separately.** Recompute every fraction operation with numerator and denominator both transformed correctly. Track the sign of every term through distribution, subtraction, transposition, and negative substitution. Check decimal point placement and magnitude against a rough estimate. Treat silent rounding, truncation, a dropped remainder, or a fractional result snapped to a whole number as an error unless the problem's context explicitly requires whole units — and check the direction of any justified rounding.

7. **Check completeness against the question asked.** The final answer must be the exact quantity requested, in the requested form or units, with all cases or parts handled. A chain that stops early, omits a case, or answers a different quantity is an error: quote the line where the reasoning terminates or diverges.

8. **One claim per error, one error per claim.** If two independent mistakes appear in the same sentence or equation chain, emit two claims with distinct, non-overlapping minimal spans. Never let one wide quote stand for two errors, and never file two claims about the same defect.

9. **Quote minimally and verbatim, at the point of introduction.** The span must be an exact contiguous substring, no longer than the clause or equation carrying the mistake, and long enough to be unique. Quote the fragment where the wrong quantity, operation, or interpretation is first written down — not a later restatement of it, and never a merge of adjacent sentences.

10. **Require a finished re-derivation, and keep the explanation short.** In two to four sentences state what the line asserts, what the correct derivation gives, and why the line is wrong. If you cannot produce the correct value or the correct relation concretely, you have a suspicion, not a claim — drop it.

11. **Do not claim non-mathematical defects.** Markup, notation style, spelling, spacing, omitted units, alternative-but-valid methods, redundant-but-harmless steps, and presentation that does not change a value are not errors. Claim only when a value, an operation, an interpretation, or a logical inference is wrong.

12. **Report every step that failed re-derivation on its own terms, and nothing else.** Do not pad to reach a count and do not trim out of caution.
